How to Choose the Right Font for Your Brand Logo

6 min read · Typography

Before a customer reads a single word of your marketing copy, the shape of your logo's letters has already told them something. Sharp and geometric feels different from round and friendly, which feels different from an elegant, thin serif. Font choice is one of the highest-leverage decisions in logo design, and it's also one of the easiest to get wrong by picking something simply because it "looks nice" rather than because it fits.

Start with the four broad families

Almost every logo typeface falls into one of four categories, and each one carries a different set of associations.

Serif fonts

The small feet at the ends of each letter stroke give serif fonts a traditional, established, trustworthy feel. They're a common choice for law firms, financial services, universities, and heritage brands — anything that wants to communicate history and reliability.

Sans-serif fonts

Clean and modern, sans-serif fonts dominate tech, startups, and consumer apps because they read as efficient and contemporary. A bold geometric sans (think evenly rounded circles and straight lines) feels confident and direct; a thinner sans feels more minimal and refined.

Script fonts

Flowing, handwriting-style letterforms suggest craft, personality, and a human touch — great for bakeries, boutiques, beauty brands, and anything that wants to feel personal rather than corporate. Use script sparingly, though; it's usually one of the hardest styles to keep legible at small sizes.

Display and decorative fonts

Bold, stylized, sometimes quirky typefaces built to grab attention. They work well for entertainment, gaming, and youth-oriented brands, but they can feel out of place anywhere that needs to read as serious or dependable.

Match the weight to the message

Beyond the family, the weight of a font — how thick or thin the strokes are — changes the tone just as much. A heavy, bold weight reads as strong and assertive; a light, thin weight reads as elegant or delicate. A fitness brand and a spa might both reasonably use a sans-serif font, but the fitness brand will usually lean bold and condensed, while the spa leans light and airy.

Legibility beats personality

A font that looks striking in a mockup but is hard to read at a glance has failed at its most basic job. Test any candidate font at the sizes it will actually be used — a business card, a favicon, a sign viewed from across a street. Watch out for:

  • Letters that are easy to confuse, like a lowercase "l" and an uppercase "I" in certain fonts
  • Tight letter spacing that makes words blur together when scaled down
  • Scripts or handwriting fonts with strokes so thin they vanish when printed small

One font is usually enough

It's tempting to pair a display font for the brand name with a second font for a tagline, and a third for good measure. In practice, one well-chosen typeface — used consistently, perhaps in two weights — almost always looks more polished than three competing styles. If you do use a second font, keep it purely functional (like a simple sans-serif for a tagline) so it doesn't compete with the primary wordmark.

Consider what your competitors are already using

Look at five or six competitors in your space. If everyone uses a similar type style, that's useful information — it tells you what customers expect from the category, and it also shows you an opportunity. Standing out by choosing a font from a different family than your competitors can make your brand instantly more distinct, as long as it doesn't contradict the tone customers expect.

When in doubt, test it in context

A font that looks perfect in a design tool can feel completely different once it's sitting on your actual homepage, printed on a real business card, or displayed on a storefront sign. Before finalizing anything, mock the logo up in as many real contexts as you can — even a rough version pasted onto a photo of a shopfront — to see how it holds up outside the design canvas.
